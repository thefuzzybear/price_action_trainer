How to read candlestick charts — a complete beginner's guide

8 min read · Price Action Basics

Candlestick charts were invented in 18th century Japan by rice traders who needed a way to visualise the relationship between a market's opening price, its highest and lowest point during the session, and where it closed. Three hundred years later they remain the dominant chart type used by professional traders worldwide — because they compress enormous amounts of information into a single visual unit.

This guide explains exactly what each part of a candlestick tells you, how to interpret the relative sizes of bodies and wicks, and the key single-candle signals that appear across every market and timeframe.

The anatomy of a single candlestick

Every candlestick represents a fixed time period — one day on a daily chart, one week on a weekly chart, one hour on an hourly chart. Within that period, four prices are recorded:

  • Open — the first traded price when the period began
  • High — the highest price reached during the period
  • Low — the lowest price reached during the period
  • Close — the last traded price before the period ended

The body of the candle is the thick rectangle drawn between the open and close. If price closed higher than it opened, the body is typically drawn in green (or white in traditional charts) — this is a bullish candle. If price closed lower than it opened, the body is drawn in red (or black) — a bearish candle.

The thin lines extending above and below the body are called wicks (also: shadows, tails). The upper wick runs from the top of the body to the high of the period. The lower wick runs from the bottom of the body to the low.

What the body tells you

The relative size of the body reveals how decisive the session's move was. A large body means buyers (bullish) or sellers (bearish) dominated the entire session with conviction — price moved strongly in one direction and stayed there. A small body means neither side won convincingly; the session ended close to where it started.

Body size is always relative. A candle that looks large on its own might be unremarkable compared to the candles around it. Context matters far more than any single bar in isolation.

What the wicks tell you — and why they matter most

Wicks are where the real story lives. A long wick reveals that price reached a certain level during the session, but was rejected from that level before the close.

A long upper wick means price pushed up to the high, but sellers stepped in aggressively and pushed it back down before the close. This is evidence of selling pressure at that level — bears defended the highs.

A long lower wick means price dropped to the low, but buyers stepped in aggressively and pushed it back up before the close. This is evidence of buying pressure at that level — bulls defended the lows.

"The wick is the evidence that someone showed up and said 'not here'."

This is why wicks into key support and resistance levels carry so much significance in price action trading. A long lower wick at a major support level tells a precise story: price tested that support, found buyers in volume, and reversed. The wick is the fingerprint of that transaction.

Important single-candle patterns

The Doji

A doji has virtually no body — open and close are at or near the same price. This means the session ended exactly where it began, regardless of how far price moved in between. A doji represents indecision: neither buyers nor sellers won the session. After a sustained trend, a doji can signal that momentum is fading.

The Hammer and Hanging Man

Both have small bodies and long lower wicks with little or no upper wick. When this pattern appears after a downtrend, it's called a hammer — the lower wick shows buyers defended a level and pushed price back up, potentially ending the down move. When the same shape appears after an uptrend, it's called a hanging man — sellers briefly took price down aggressively before buyers recovered it, but the attempt is a warning sign.

The Shooting Star and Inverted Hammer

The mirror of the hammer: small body, long upper wick, little or no lower wick. After an uptrend, a shooting star shows that buyers pushed price up strongly during the session, but sellers rejected the highs and closed price back near the open — a bearish signal. After a downtrend, the same shape is an inverted hammer — a potential bullish reversal signal if confirmed by the next candle.

The Engulfing pattern

Technically a two-candle pattern, but worth including because of its importance. A bullish engulfing pattern occurs when a bearish candle is followed by a bullish candle whose body completely engulfs the previous body — buyers overwhelmed sellers so completely that they erased the previous session's losses and more. A bearish engulfing is the inverse.

Key principle: No single candlestick pattern means anything in isolation. The pattern's significance depends entirely on where it appears in the broader structure — at a key level, after a trend, at a volume node. Context is everything.

Colour is just convention

Green and red (or white and black) are the most common colour schemes, but they're just display conventions. What matters is the relationship between open and close, not the colour. Some traders use non-standard colours deliberately to avoid emotional responses to "red candles" — the data is neutral.

Practising candlestick reading

Reading candlesticks fluently is a physical skill, like reading music. You build it through repetition and immediate feedback — not through studying patterns on a page.

The most effective way to develop this skill is to step through historical charts bar by bar, making a prediction before each bar reveals, and reviewing the outcome immediately. This forces you to read the current structure without knowing what comes next — exactly the same cognitive challenge as live trading.

Price action vs technical indicators — why traders go bare chart

10 min read · Methodology

Open any trading forum and you'll find beginners asking which combination of indicators gives the best signals. RSI plus MACD plus Bollinger Bands. Moving average crossovers. Stochastic with a filter. The question is reasonable — indicators look like an objective, scientific way to remove emotion from the analysis.

But among consistently profitable professional traders, there's a notable tendency to use fewer indicators over time, not more. Many use none at all. This article explains why.

What indicators actually are

Every technical indicator is, at its core, a mathematical transformation of price data. The RSI is a function of recent price changes. The MACD is the difference between two exponential moving averages of price. Bollinger Bands are a moving average with standard deviation envelopes around it.

This matters because it means indicators are derived from price. They cannot contain information that isn't already present in the raw price data — they simply reorganise that information into a different visual form. The question, then, is whether that reorganisation makes decisions clearer or less clear.

The lag problem

Most popular indicators are based on moving averages in some form, which means they are inherently backwards-looking. A 20-period moving average is the average of the last 20 closes. By the time a crossover signal fires, price has already moved — the signal is confirming what already happened, not predicting what comes next.

This is called lag, and it's not a flaw that better settings can eliminate. It's structural. Longer periods produce smoother signals with less noise, but more lag. Shorter periods reduce lag but produce more false signals. There's no free lunch.

"Indicators tell you what price did. Price action tells you what the market is doing."

The clutter problem

Beyond lag, indicator-heavy charts create a second problem: they hide the most important price action signals beneath layers of lines. Long lower wicks at key support levels, compression before a breakout, failed breakdowns — these patterns are immediately visible on a clean chart and much harder to see when the price bars themselves are buried under four oscillators and a band of moving averages.

There's also a psychological dimension. When a chart has six indicators and price is at a key level, the trader's attention fragments. They look for the RSI to confirm, but the RSI is lagging. They wait for the MACD to cross, but by the time it does the move is over. The indicators become a reason to hesitate rather than a tool to clarify.

What price action traders read instead

A clean price action chart has two primary inputs: candlesticks and price levels. The analysis is built from:

Market structure

Is price making higher highs and higher lows (uptrend), lower highs and lower lows (downtrend), or oscillating between a range? Structure is the first question asked before any entry is considered.

Key horizontal levels

Price tends to react at levels where it has reacted before. Previous swing highs become resistance. Previous swing lows become support. The horizontal line drawn at an obvious level on a daily chart is often more useful than any oscillator reading.

Candlestick behaviour at levels

When price approaches a key level, how does it arrive? Does it come in with wide, decisive candles suggesting momentum? Or with small, compressed candles suggesting exhaustion? Does it penetrate the level with a large body, or wick through it and close back below? The candlestick behaviour at the level is the signal — not an RSI reading.

Volume context

Volume is the one indicator many pure price action traders keep — not as a mechanical signal, but as context. A breakout on high volume is more credible than one on thin volume. A rejection wick that forms on high volume suggests genuine supply or demand.

Does this mean indicators are useless?

No. Indicators are tools, and some traders use them effectively. The issue is the order of priority. When an indicator conflicts with a clear price action signal, which do you follow? Most experienced traders follow price. If that's the case, the indicator is secondary information at best and noise at worst.

The most honest test is to ask: if I removed all my indicators and just read the price structure, would I make better or worse decisions? For most traders who've gone through the process of learning price action, the answer is better.

A useful exercise: Take any chart you normally trade with indicators. Look at the last 20 signals your indicator generated. Then look at the raw price structure at those same points. Which told the cleaner story?

Building the skill

Reading price action without indicators is a learnable skill, but it requires deliberate practice rather than passive observation. The challenge is that most traders watch charts without committing to a prediction before the bar reveals — which means they're subject to hindsight bias. Everything looks obvious in retrospect.

The antidote is to practice making explicit predictions before the next bar forms, tracking your accuracy, and reviewing where and why your reads failed. This is exactly the kind of feedback loop that builds real skill.
